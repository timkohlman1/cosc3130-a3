\section*{Research Summary}

Misinformation on social media spreads through images, memes, and out-of-context photographs as readily as through words — yet the strongest automated detection models still read only text. The Multimodal and Context-Aware Ensemble (MACE) framework addresses this by making one precise change to established detection models: replacing the text-only content branch in DANES and GETAE with a multimodal branch capable of processing both text and images, while leaving the social-context branch — which captures how information propagates through networks — completely untouched. Because only the content branch changes, any improvement in detection performance can be attributed directly to the visual input, rather than to other architectural modifications. Three research questions structure the investigation: whether this visual upgrade improves detection and at what computational cost; how much each branch contributes independently; and which datasets are genuinely suited for this type of evaluation.

\section{Introduction}

The widespread circulation of misinformation has created significant challenges for information credibility, public trust, and informed decision-making. The use of social media platforms enables the rapid creation, sharing, and resharing of information, allowing false or misleading content to reach large audiences before verification or correction can occur. Recent evidence demonstrates the scale of this issue. The World Economic Forum identifies misinformation and disinformation as major short-term global risks due to their potential impact on social cohesion, institutional trust, and public decision-making \citep{WEF2025}. Similarly, the \textit{Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2025} reports that 58\% of surveyed users remain concerned about their ability to distinguish true information from false information online, while the use of social and video-based platforms for news consumption continues to increase \citep{Newman2025}. In Australia, the Australian Communications and Media Authority reported that 72\% of adult digital platform users believed they had encountered misinformation during the first half of 2025 \citep{ACMA2025}. These findings demonstrate that misinformation detection is not only a technical classification problem, but also an important real-world issue affecting public trust, platform safety, and social resilience.

The propagation behaviour of misinformation further supports the need for effective automated detection. A large-scale analysis of online news diffusion found that false news was 70\% more likely to be retweeted than true news and reached 1,500 people approximately six times faster than truthful information \citep{Vosoughi2018}. This finding indicates that the detection of inauthentic content requires consideration beyond the textual claim itself. The visual presentation of a post, the interaction patterns surrounding it, and the way it spreads through online networks are also important detection signals. Therefore, the detection problem becomes more complex in social media environments where misleading information can be communicated through text, images, comments, reposts, user interactions, and propagation patterns.

Despite these developments, a clear research gap remains. Existing multimodal models provide improvement in content representation, but often include limited integration of propagation-based social context. Existing context-aware models such as DANES and GETAE achieve strong performance through the use of social and textual signals, but their content branch remains text-focused and therefore does not fully capture visual misinformation. Recent studies have started to combine multimodal content and social context, including EFND, DPSG, and the framework proposed by Dellys et al. \citep{Nadeem2023,jing2025,Dellys2025}. However, these studies use different social branch designs, engagement signals, dynamic graph structures, or fusion strategies. This variation makes the attribution of performance gains difficult, because improvement may result from multimodal content, social context, or other architectural changes. As a result, the specific effect of replacing a text-only content branch with a multimodal branch inside a DANES/GETAE-style backbone remains insufficiently understood.

A second gap concerns computational cost. The inclusion of visual encoders and multimodal fusion can support detection improvement, but it can also increase inference time, memory usage, and computational resource requirements. For real-world deployment, improvement in accuracy is only meaningful if the additional cost can be justified. Therefore, this research does not only examine whether multimodal substitution improves detection performance, but also whether the level of improvement justifies the associated computational overhead.

A third gap concerns dataset adaptation and generalisability. The datasets used by DANES and GETAE, such as Twitter15 and Twitter16, provide useful propagation structures but limited multimodal content. In contrast, datasets such as FakeNewsNet and MuMin provide richer multimodal and social context data, but they also introduce practical limitations such as missing or broken image URLs, sparse propagation traces, and class imbalance \citep{shu2020fakenewsnet,mumindataset}. Without the establishment of explicit dataset adaptation criteria, model performance may reflect dataset artefacts rather than genuine improvement in misinformation detection. This creates a need for transparent preprocessing requirements, image availability checks, graph-density thresholds, and class-balance considerations before the interpretation of model results.

This research addresses these gaps through the development of MACE, a context-aware multimodal misinformation detection framework. The study investigates three connected research questions. First, it examines whether the replacement of the text-only content branch in DANES/GETAE-style models with a multimodal content branch improves detection performance, and what computational cost is associated with this change. Second, it evaluates the relative contribution of the multimodal content branch and the social context branch through controlled ablation. Third, it examines the criteria and preprocessing steps required for the adaptation of existing multimodal social media datasets to the DANES/GETAE ensemble backbone, and how dataset choice affects the generalisability of results.

The contribution of this research is fourfold. First, it extends existing context-aware misinformation detection architectures through the incorporation of a multimodal content branch. Second, it introduces a controlled substitution design that allows performance changes to be more clearly attributed to the replacement of the content branch. Third, it uses ablation-based evaluation for the separation of the contribution of multimodal content from the contribution of social context. Fourth, it contributes a dataset adaptation perspective through the identification of conditions under which multimodal social media datasets can be reliably used for MACE evaluation. Together, these contributions support the development of misinformation detection systems that are more aligned with the textual, visual, and interaction-based nature of real-world social media content.
