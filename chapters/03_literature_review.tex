\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Overview of Social Media Misinformation Detection}

The development of misinformation detection in social media can be observed across three main directions: text-based content analysis, social-context modelling, and multimodal detection. Earlier studies framed misinformation detection as a content classification problem, focusing on the linguistic and semantic properties of posts. While these approaches established useful baselines, increasing limitations have been identified in modern social media environments, where misleading information is conveyed not only through text but also through images, memes, screenshots, and user interaction patterns. As a result, research has gradually shifted from text-only classification toward multimodal and context-aware modelling, as these approaches are better able to capture the wider range of signals available on social media platforms. In general, text-based methods provide a useful semantic baseline, multimodal methods improve content representation, and context-aware approaches capture behavioural signals that are not available from content alone \citep{Wang2018,Qian2021,truica2024danes,truica2025getae}.

\section{Text-Only Content-Based Detection Approaches}

Text-only content modelling remains one of the foundational approaches in misinformation detection and is still used within many later systems. Its main strength is that it provides a strong semantic baseline, especially when misleading claims are expressed clearly through written language. This is particularly important in current social media environments, where misleading content is often presented through images, memes, screenshots, or visual-text combinations rather than text alone. Compared with later multimodal and context-aware approaches, text-only modelling captures a much narrower view of social media posts, and its limitations are increasingly evident in cases where misinformation is conveyed through visual and multimodal formats. This is visible in models such as DANES and GETAE, where text-only representations are still used within the content branch despite strong performance \citep{truica2024danes,truica2025getae}. Therefore, while text-only modelling is still useful as a baseline, it is increasingly limited as a standalone approach for detecting misinformation in modern social media settings.

\section{Context-Aware Detection with Social and Propagation Signals}

A major development beyond text-only detection is the incorporation of social context into misinformation detection. Unlike text-only approaches, these methods model information spread, user engagement, and temporal interaction patterns, allowing them to capture behavioural evidence that is not visible from content alone. Studies show that diffusion patterns, user relationships, and temporal dynamics provide strong classification signals that are not available from content alone \citep{Song2021,Min2022,Zhu24}. Models such as DANES and GETAE represent important context-aware ensemble architectures, combining a content branch with a social or propagation branch. Their main advantage over content-only approaches is the ability to use structured social and propagation signals, which often improves detection performance. However, because their content branch often remains text-only, these models still struggle to capture misinformation that is conveyed visually. Additionally, propagation-based methods depend on interaction data that may be sparse or delayed, particularly in early detection scenarios. Consequently, while detection extends beyond content, limitations of text-only representation are still inherited \citep{truica2024danes,truica2025getae}.

\section{Multimodal Content-Based Detection Approaches}

Compared with text-only modelling, multimodal detection is better suited to the way misinformation is presented on modern social media, where meaning is often shared across both text and images. Early work such as EANN showed that combining textual and visual features can improve detection performance compared with unimodal baselines [Wang et al., 2018]. Later studies moved beyond simple fusion and focused more on how relationships between text and image can be modelled more effectively. This includes work on text-image consistency, hierarchical attention, and progressive fusion, which shows that multimodal improvement depends not only on including multiple modalities, but also on how well cross-modal interactions are learned \citep{Xue2021,Qian2021,Jing2023}. This progression indicates that gains depend not only on modality inclusion but also on interaction quality. Benchmark datasets such as Fakeddit further show that multimodal classifiers outperform unimodal baselines \citep{nakamura2020fakeddit,segura2022multimodal}. Although this literature better reflects the way misinformation appears in modern social media posts, it still focuses mainly on content-level modelling. Compared with context-aware approaches, it often makes limited use of social, behavioural, and propagation-based information.

\section{Integrating Multimodal Content and Social Context}

More recent research has tried to combine the strengths of multimodal content modelling and context-aware detection within unified frameworks. Studies such as EFND, C\textsuperscript{2}DCMTN, DPSG, TMEF-BI, and ESDGFN show that combining textual, visual, and contextual signals can achieve strong performance \citep{nadeem2023efnd,Wang2024,jing2025dpsg,Chen25,Sun25,Ji2026LDPG}. These findings suggest that multimodal content and social context are complementary, because multimodal learning improves content representation while social-context modelling captures behavioural and interaction-based evidence. However, variation in how social context is represented introduces challenges in comparison. Some models use engagement data, while others incorporate behaviour, stance, or dynamic graph fusion \citep{nadeem2023efnd,Wang2024,Chen25,Sun25}. However, this variation also makes comparison more difficult, because both the multimodal and contextual parts of the models are often changed at the same time. This makes it hard to tell whether performance gains come from improved content representation, stronger context modelling, or the interaction between both. As a result, while joint modelling is supported, the specific contribution of multimodality remains unclear, particularly compared to DANES and GETAE architectures \citep{truica2024danes,truica2025getae,dellys2025multimodal}. Overall, the literature suggests that neither content-only nor context-only modelling is fully sufficient on its own, which explains the growing interest in integrated approaches.

\section{Research Gap and Positioning of the Proposed Study}

A central gap in the literature is identified. Prior research shows that both multimodal content and social context improve misinformation detection \citep{Wang2018,Qian2021,truica2024danes,nadeem2023efnd,jing2025dpsg}. In other words, many existing studies compare completely different architectures, rather than isolating the effect of replacing a text-only content branch with a multimodal one under the same contextual conditions. As a result, the specific contribution of multimodal substitution within DANES and GETAE remains unclear, and the relative contribution of content representation and social context is still not clearly separated \citep{truica2024danes,truica2025getae,dellys2025multimodal,Chen25}. This gap aligns with the project’s research questions. Research Question 1 evaluates performance improvement from multimodal substitution, while Research Question 2 examines whether multimodal and social branches provide complementary or overlapping value. To address this, the study uses DANES and GETAE as baselines, replaces the text-only content branch with a multimodal alternative, and conducts ablation-based comparisons across configurations. This enables a controlled evaluation of the contribution of multimodal content and social context in misinformation detection.
