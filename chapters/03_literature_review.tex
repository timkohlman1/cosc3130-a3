\section{Overview of Social Media Misinformation Detection}

Research into social media misinformation detection has developed across several related directions, including text-based content modelling, multimodal content analysis, and social-context or propagation-based modelling. Earlier approaches commonly treated misinformation detection as a supervised classification problem, where the main task was to classify a post, article, or claim as authentic or inauthentic based on textual information. These approaches provided an important foundation because they showed that misinformation often contains identifiable linguistic, semantic, or stylistic patterns \citep{Wang2018,Song2021,truica2024danes}.

However, the modern social media environment has made the detection problem more complex. Inauthentic content is no longer communicated only through written claims. It is often created using images, memes, screenshots, edited visual material, reused photographs, misleading captions, user engagement, comments, reposts, and propagation patterns. This means that a detection model that only analyses written text may miss important evidence carried by visual or social signals \citep{Wang2018,Nakamura2020,Qian2021}.

As a result, recent misinformation detection research increasingly recognises that no single signal is sufficient. Textual content can capture the claim being made, visual content can capture supporting or misleading evidence, and social context can capture how the information spreads and how users interact with it. The studies summarised in Table~\ref{tab:lit_summary} show this progression clearly. Text-only models remain useful as baselines, multimodal models improve content representation, and social-context models capture diffusion and interaction behaviour. However, the table also shows a recurring limitation: many studies improve one part of the detection problem while leaving other parts under-explored.

This limitation is central to the proposed MACE framework. Existing multimodal models show that text and images are useful together, while DANES and GETAE show that social-context modelling improves detection \citep{Qian2021,truica2024danes,GETAE2025}. However, there is still limited work that performs a controlled replacement of the text-only content branch inside a DANES/GETAE-style propagation ensemble while keeping the social branch, fusion mechanism, and classifier largely unchanged. Therefore, the literature review focuses not only on what previous studies achieved, but also on the methodological limitations that create the gap for this research.

\section{Text-Only Content-Based Detection Approaches}

Text-only content-based approaches remain an important starting point in fake news and misinformation detection. These models represent posts, articles, or claims using linguistic and semantic features and then classify them based on textual evidence. Earlier methods used hand-crafted linguistic features, while later approaches adopted neural models such as convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, and transformer-based encoders such as BERT \citep{Devlin2019,truica2024danes}.

The main strength of text-only approaches is that they provide a controlled and computationally efficient baseline. Since they only process text, they avoid the additional complexity of image collection, image preprocessing, visual feature extraction, and multimodal fusion. This makes them useful for establishing whether language alone provides enough evidence for detection. Text-only models are also easier to reproduce because textual inputs are usually more stable than images or social media interaction data, which may be deleted or become unavailable over time.

However, the limitation of text-only detection is increasingly significant in current social media settings. Many misleading posts rely on visual persuasion, including manipulated images, misleading screenshots, memes, reused photographs, or images taken out of context. In these cases, the written text may be short, neutral, or incomplete, while the misleading meaning is created through the relationship between the text and the image. A text-only model may therefore fail to detect misinformation where the visual component carries the deceptive signal.

This limitation also applies to DANES and GETAE. Both models are strong context-aware baselines because they combine textual content with social-context or propagation information. However, their content branches remain text-only \citep{truica2024danes,GETAE2025}. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:lit_summary}, DANES and GETAE achieve strong performance through the combination of content and social context, but they still inherit the weakness of not explicitly modelling visual misinformation. This provides the basis for RQ1, which asks whether replacing the text-only content branch with a multimodal content branch improves detection performance within the same DANES/GETAE-style backbone.

Methodologically, text-only models are valuable because they provide a clean baseline for comparison. However, their findings are limited when applied to multimodal social media environments. Their performance may reflect the strength of textual cues in specific datasets rather than a complete ability to detect real-world misinformation. For this reason, the proposed MACE framework treats text-only DANES/GETAE performance as the baseline rather than the final solution.

\section{Context-Aware Detection with Social and Propagation Signals}

A second major research direction focuses on social context and propagation behaviour. These approaches are based on the idea that misinformation differs not only in what it says, but also in how it spreads, who interacts with it, how quickly it is reshared, and what type of engagement pattern it creates \citep{Vosoughi2018,Song2021,Min2022}. Social-context models therefore use features such as user interactions, repost structures, propagation trees, temporal diffusion, graph relationships, and engagement behaviour.

Propagation-based models are important because misinformation often spreads through structured social networks. For example, temporally evolving graph neural network approaches show that the timing and structure of diffusion can provide useful classification evidence. Post-user interaction models also show that relationships between users and posts can improve detection by capturing behavioural patterns that are not visible from the content alone. More recent propagation-aware graph transformer models further demonstrate that graph structure can improve robustness and interpretability in misinformation detection \citep{Song2021,Min2022,Zhu24}.

DANES and GETAE are particularly important for this research because they represent strong context-aware ensemble baselines. DANES combines a text-based content branch with a social-context branch, while GETAE extends this idea through graph information and propagation-enhanced embeddings \citep{truica2024danes,GETAE2025}. Their dual-branch structure is valuable because it separates content evidence from social-context evidence before combining them for classification. This makes them suitable backbones for the proposed MACE framework.

However, social-context and propagation-based approaches also have methodological limitations. Their performance depends heavily on the availability and quality of interaction data. In early detection scenarios, a post may not yet have enough reposts, comments, or propagation traces to form a reliable social graph. In other cases, user accounts, posts, or media may have been deleted, which can result in incomplete graph structures. This means that strong performance on one dataset may not transfer to another dataset with different graph density or user engagement patterns.

This limitation directly supports RQ3. Since MACE depends on both multimodal content and social-context information, dataset suitability cannot be assumed. Candidate datasets must be checked for graph density, user or propagation traces, missing media, class balance, and computational feasibility before they can be used for controlled evaluation. Therefore, the literature suggests that social-context modelling is valuable, but only when the dataset provides enough reliable interaction evidence.

\section{Multimodal Content-Based Detection Approaches}

Multimodal misinformation detection addresses the limitations of text-only approaches by combining textual and visual features. EANN is one of the foundational models in this area. It demonstrated that combining text and images can improve fake news detection compared with text-only or image-only baselines. The importance of multimodal learning was further supported by datasets such as Fakeddit, which showed that multimodal representations can improve classification across different fake news categories \citep{Wang2018,Nakamura2020}.

Later models moved beyond simply adding image features and focused more on how text and images should be fused. MCNN showed that modelling consistency between text and image is important because fake news often uses mismatched or misleading visual evidence. HMCAN introduced hierarchical multimodal contextual attention, allowing the model to capture relationships between textual and visual features at different levels. MPFN further demonstrated that progressive fusion across multiple feature levels can improve multimodal representation quality \citep{Xue2021,Qian2021,Jing2023}.

For this study, HMCAN is especially important because it is selected as the multimodal content branch in the MACE methodology. HMCAN uses BERT to extract textual features and ResNet-50 to extract visual features, before applying multimodal contextual attention and hierarchical encoding \citep{Devlin2019,He2016,Qian2021}. This makes it suitable for MACE because it produces a fixed-length multimodal post representation that can replace the original text-only content embedding used in the DANES/GETAE-style ensemble. Unlike MPFN, HMCAN is not treated as a fallback option in the updated methodology. MPFN remains relevant as related work because it shows the importance of fusion design, but it is not the selected branch for MACE.

A key methodological strength of multimodal content models is that they directly test the value of adding visual information. However, many of these models remain content-focused and do not include structured propagation or social-context modelling. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:lit_summary}, multimodal-only models such as EANN, MCNN, HMCAN, and MPFN generally improve content representation, but they do not fully answer how multimodal content behaves when placed inside a propagation-based ensemble such as DANES or GETAE.

Another important issue is visual encoder selection. Earlier multimodal models often used CNN-based visual encoders such as VGG or ResNet, while more recent approaches increasingly use transformer-based encoders such as ViT or vision-language models such as CLIP \citep{He2016,Dosovitskiy2021,Radford2021}. ResNet-50 is a defensible choice for MACE because it is widely used, computationally established, and already part of the HMCAN pipeline. However, newer encoders such as ViT and CLIP may provide stronger image-text alignment. This means the visual encoder choice should be treated as a methodological decision rather than an automatic assumption.

For MACE, the purpose is not to optimise the visual encoder itself. Instead, the purpose is to test whether replacing the original text-only content branch with an HMCAN-based multimodal branch improves performance while keeping the rest of the DANES/GETAE-style ensemble controlled. Therefore, using HMCAN provides a practical balance between multimodal capability, reproducibility, and compatibility with the proposed controlled experimental design.

\section{Integrating Multimodal Content and Social Context}

Recent research has increasingly attempted to combine multimodal content and social-context information within unified fake news detection frameworks. This direction is highly relevant to MACE because it recognises that misinformation detection benefits from both content-level evidence and social behaviour. However, existing integrated models differ significantly in how they represent social context and how they fuse text, image, and social signals.

EFND is one of the earlier models that explicitly combines semantic, visual, and socially augmented features \citep{Nadeem2023}. It demonstrates that textual, visual, and social signals can be complementary. However, its social context is based on engagement-related and socially augmented signals rather than the DANES/GETAE-style propagation-graph ensemble structure. Therefore, while EFND supports the value of combining content and context, it does not directly test whether replacing only the text branch inside DANES or GETAE improves performance.

Wang et al.'s Comment-Context Dual Collaborative Masked Transformer Network also supports the value of integrating multimodal content with comment-based social context \citep{Wang2024}. Its contribution is important because it recognises that both content and social comments may contain noise, and it attempts to filter irrelevant signals through masked transformer-based modelling. However, comment-context modelling is different from preserving a DANES/GETAE propagation branch. This makes it useful as a related multimodal-social model, but not as a direct controlled comparison for MACE.

DPSG is one of the closest recent studies to the proposed research because it combines multimodal content with dynamic propagation social graphs \citep{jing2025}. It shows that dynamic graph modelling and multimodal features can improve fake news detection. However, DPSG changes both the multimodal representation and the graph modelling strategy. This means its reported improvement cannot be attributed only to multimodal content, because the social branch and fusion strategy also differ from DANES/GETAE. Methodologically, this is the key difference between DPSG and MACE. DPSG aims to build a stronger integrated model, while MACE aims to isolate the effect of multimodal substitution inside an established ensemble backbone.

Dellys et al. also directly examine the integration of social context into multimodal fake news detection \citep{Dellys2025}. Their work is highly relevant because it demonstrates that adding social context to multimodal detection can improve performance. However, their approach uses a different fusion design and does not fully isolate the effect of replacing the text-only branch in a DANES/GETAE-style architecture. Therefore, it supports the motivation for MACE but does not answer the same research question.

TMEF-BI introduces another important direction by treating multimodal fusion as a reliability-aware process \citep{Chen2025}. Instead of assuming that all modalities are equally useful, it estimates uncertainty and weights modality contributions based on reliability. This is methodologically valuable because different posts may rely more strongly on text, image, or behaviour signals. However, its behaviour-based social signal differs from the propagation-graph social branch used in DANES and GETAE. Therefore, its findings cannot be directly transferred to the MACE setting.

Sun et al.'s ESDGFN and Ji et al.'s LDPG further show the recent movement toward dynamic, graph-based, multimodal detection \citep{Sun25,Ji2026LDPG}. ESDGFN combines multimodal source content with evolving stance and dynamic conversation graphs, while LDPG uses local dynamic propagation graphs to reduce noise and improve early detection. These models are important because they show the current direction of the field: combining multimodal content with increasingly sophisticated temporal and social graph modelling. However, they also reinforce the methodological problem that MACE addresses. Many recent models change several components at once, including the text encoder, visual encoder, graph structure, fusion mechanism, and classifier. As a result, the exact source of performance improvement becomes difficult to isolate.

This is where MACE is positioned differently. MACE does not attempt to redesign every part of the detection pipeline. Instead, it retains the DANES/GETAE-style social-context branch, fusion mechanism, and classifier as far as possible, while replacing only the original text-only content branch with an HMCAN-based multimodal branch. This controlled substitution design directly supports RQ1 and RQ2. RQ1 evaluates whether the multimodal substitution improves performance and whether the improvement justifies the added computational cost. RQ2 uses ablation conditions to examine how much the multimodal content branch and social-context branch each contribute to the final prediction.

\section{Computational Cost and Practical Deployment Considerations}

Although multimodal models can improve detection performance, they usually introduce additional computational cost. A text-only model typically requires one main text encoder, such as BERT. In contrast, an HMCAN-style multimodal pipeline requires both a text encoder and a visual encoder, as well as additional preprocessing and fusion operations. The updated MACE methodology specifically uses HMCAN with BERT and ResNet-50, meaning that the model must process both textual and visual inputs before producing a multimodal content representation \citep{Qian2021,Devlin2019,He2016}.

This issue matters because misinformation detection may need to operate in time-sensitive environments. A model that improves F1-score only slightly may not be practical if it significantly increases inference time, GPU memory usage, or storage requirements. Many fake news detection studies report accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, but fewer studies report computational cost in a consistent way. This limits the practical interpretation of results because a model that performs well in a controlled experiment may be difficult to deploy at scale.

The MACE methodology addresses this limitation by treating computational cost as part of the research problem rather than as a minor implementation detail. The study proposes to report training time, inference time, peak memory usage, storage requirements, and hardware configuration alongside standard classification metrics \citep{Mattson2020}. This is important because the comparison between the original DANES/GETAE baseline and the MACE variant should not only ask whether multimodal substitution improves performance, but whether the performance improvement is worth the additional cost.

This also strengthens RQ1. Instead of only asking whether multimodal substitution improves accuracy, RQ1 asks what cost is associated with this substitution in terms of inference time and computational resource usage. This makes the research more practically relevant because it considers deployment feasibility as well as classification performance.

\section{Dataset Characteristics and Selection Challenges}

Dataset selection is a major methodological challenge in misinformation detection. Different datasets vary in platform source, language, topic, label construction, modality availability, social-context completeness, class balance, and preprocessing requirements. These differences affect model performance and make cross-study comparison difficult \citep{Nakamura2020,shu2020fakenewsnet,mumindataset}. For MACE, dataset selection is especially important because the framework requires both multimodal content and social or propagation information.

Twitter15 and Twitter16 are useful for Stage 1 replication because they are commonly used in rumour and fake news detection and provide propagation structures suitable for DANES and GETAE. However, they are less suitable for evaluating the full MACE framework because they do not provide the same level of multimodal content as newer datasets. This means they are appropriate for validating the baseline implementation but not sufficient for answering all parts of RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3.

FakeNewsNet and MuMin are more relevant candidate datasets for MACE because they provide richer multimodal and social-context information \citep{shu2020fakenewsnet,mumindataset}. FakeNewsNet includes news content, social context, and spatiotemporal information, making it suitable for multimodal and context-aware fake news detection. However, it may include missing media, unavailable posts, broken image links, or incomplete social traces. MuMin is a large-scale multilingual multimodal dataset that includes tweets, users, fact-checked claims, and graph relationships. Its richness makes it valuable for evaluating multimodal and social-context models, but its scale and graph complexity may create preprocessing and computational challenges \citep{mumindataset,Chiang2019}.

Therefore, FakeNewsNet and MuMin should not be assumed automatically usable. The updated methodology correctly treats them as candidate datasets that must pass Stage 2 adaptation and feasibility checks before being used in Stage 3 experiments. These checks include record count, image availability, missing or broken media links, label compatibility, graph density, class balance, user or propagation trace availability, memory requirements, and storage requirements.

Class imbalance is another important issue. Misinformation datasets often inherit imbalance from fact-checking sources or platform sampling strategies. If class imbalance is not handled properly, accuracy may become misleading because a model can perform well by favouring the majority class. This supports the use of macro-F1, precision, recall, and class-specific analysis rather than accuracy alone.

Cross-dataset generalisation is also a major concern. A model trained or evaluated on one dataset may not perform equally well on another because datasets differ in topics, platforms, languages, visual styles, label definitions, user behaviours, and propagation structures. For MACE, this means that a performance improvement on one adapted dataset may not automatically generalise. RQ3 therefore asks what dataset criteria and preprocessing steps are required, and how dataset choice affects the generalisability of results.

The inclusion of Stage 2 dataset adaptation is therefore an important methodological contribution. It ensures that dataset suitability is evaluated before model results are interpreted. This reduces the risk of treating dataset artefacts as model improvements and makes the proposed evaluation more transparent and reproducible.

\section{Research Gap and Positioning of the Proposed Study}

The reviewed literature shows that misinformation detection has advanced through text-only modelling, multimodal content learning, social-context modelling, and integrated multimodal-social frameworks. However, a clear gap remains in the controlled integration of multimodal content into established propagation-based ensemble architectures.

Text-only models provide useful and efficient baselines, but they miss visual misinformation. Multimodal content models improve text-image representation, but they often exclude structured social context. Context-aware models such as DANES and GETAE incorporate social and propagation signals, but their content branches remain text-only. Recent multimodal-social models such as EFND, DPSG, Dellys et al., TMEF-BI, Sun et al., and Ji et al. demonstrate the value of combining content and context, but they often change several architectural components at once \citep{Nadeem2023,jing2025,Dellys2025,Chen2025,Sun25,Ji2026LDPG}.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:lit_summary}, this creates a recurring methodological limitation. Multimodal-social models report strong performance, but because they frequently change the visual encoder, text encoder, graph construction, fusion mechanism, and classifier simultaneously, it is difficult to identify the specific source of improvement. In particular, existing studies do not clearly answer whether replacing only the text-only content branch inside a DANES/GETAE-style propagation ensemble improves performance when the social branch and classifier are held constant.

The novelty of MACE is therefore not simply that it combines text, image, and social context. Its contribution is the controlled substitution of the original DANES/GETAE text-only content branch with an HMCAN-based multimodal branch while retaining the original social-context branch, fusion mechanism, and classifier as far as possible \citep{truica2024danes,GETAE2025,Qian2021}. This design allows performance changes to be attributed more directly to multimodal content substitution rather than to unrelated architectural changes.

The proposed study also extends prior work by treating computational cost and dataset suitability as explicit research concerns. By reporting inference time, training time, peak memory usage, and storage requirements, MACE evaluates whether multimodal substitution is practically justified. By introducing Stage 2 dataset adaptation, it also examines whether datasets such as FakeNewsNet and MuMin are suitable for controlled multimodal and propagation-based evaluation \citep{shu2020fakenewsnet,mumindataset}. Therefore, MACE contributes both architecturally and methodologically to context-aware multimodal misinformation detection.
